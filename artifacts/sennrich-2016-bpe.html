Stage 2.5 · Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, ACL 2016

BPE: Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units

Our implementation, Table 1 on WMT14 French, and BPE vs word-level RNNsearch-50 on newstest2014.

Pain point

A fixed word vocabulary turns every rare word into <UNK>

NMT in 2015 used 30-50k words per side. Everything else became <UNK>. The baselines patched this with a back-off dictionary on the aligned source word, which breaks on compounds, names and morphology. Those words translate through units smaller than a word.

Why not characters?

Characters never give <UNK>, but sequences get much longer. On our French train data, characters need 4.65x the tokens of words. Every recurrent step and every attention row gets that much longer.

The frequency criterion

Merge the most frequent adjacent pair, again and again. Common words end up as one symbol. Rare words stay split, because their pieces are more frequent than the whole word.

The merge count is the knob: 0 merges = characters, all merges = words. The paper uses 59,500 (BPE-60k); we use 30,000 per side.

Zero <UNK>

In the worst case a word falls back to characters, and characters are in the vocabulary. So the test set has no <UNK>, except for characters never seen in training (the paper's footnote: 32 UNK with joint BPE).

What the paper actually claims

Linguistic splitters (compound splitting, Morfessor, hyphenation) appear only in the Table 1 statistics, rejected because they still leave UNKs. The NMT comparisons (Table 2) are word-level, back-off dictionary, character bigrams and BPE. Whether linguistically meaningful splits translate better is not tested.

Algorithm

Count pairs, merge the top pair, recount

Start from characters plus an end-of-word marker </w>, so merges never cross a word boundary. Pair counts are weighted by word frequency. The output is an ordered list of merges.

Worked example

Word counts: low ×5, lower ×2, newest ×6, widest ×3. Each row is one merge and the corpus after it. Ties are broken by first occurrence.

1e + s → es (9)l o w </w> · l o w e r </w> · n e w es t </w> · w i d es t </w>
2es + t → est (9)n e w est </w> · w i d est </w>
3est + </w> (9)n e w est</w> · w i d est</w>
4l + o → lo (7)lo w </w> · lo w e r </w>
5lo + w → low (7)low </w> · low e r </w>
6-8n+e, ne+w, new+est</w> (6)newest</w> is now one symbol; widest stays w i d est</w>

The frequent word newest becomes one symbol. The rarer widest stays as pieces, and its suffix est</w> is shared with newest.

Applying merges to a new word

Unseen word lowest: l o w e s t </w>. Apply merges in learned order:

e+s     -> l o w es t </w>
es+t    -> l o w est </w>
est+</w> -> l o w est</w>
l+o     -> lo w est</w>
lo+w    -> low est</w>

Result: low + est</w>. Both pieces are in the vocabulary, so no <UNK>.

Rank-based apply (Vikas's rewrite)

Looping over all 30k merges per word is slow. Instead: build rules_map = {pair: rank} once; for the word's current pairs, pick the one with the lowest rank; merge; repeat until no pair is in the map.

Same output as the ordered loop, about 60x faster. Fixed two bugs on the way: a crash on a word with no pairs left, and rebuilding the map on every call.

Learning fast

Recounting all pairs after every merge is O(corpus) per merge. Our learn_bpe keeps a pair-count heap plus an index pair → words containing it. A merge only touches the words that contain the pair: remove their old pairs, add their new pairs.

Undo before scoring

The decoder emits subwords. undo_bpe joins pieces until one ends in </w>, then strips the marker. BLEU is computed on the rejoined words, so BPE and word-level scores are comparable.

Read it like this

Words: short sequences, a huge type count, and UNKs. Characters: no UNKs but 4.65x longer. BPE gets both good properties: almost word-length sequences (1.08x) and zero UNK, with a 30k vocabulary.

Only 917 UNK here because this column counts words never seen in train. With a 30k word vocabulary, everything outside the top 30k is also <UNK>: that is 6.1% of newstest2014 target tokens.

Small-corpus artifact

On a 2k-sentence Mac slice, BPE showed more UNK (7,748) than whole words. Not a code bug: 30k merges exhaust a ~5,200-word vocabulary after 9,018 merges, so every train word becomes one symbol, and unseen test words split into pieces never seen in train. On the full data BPE UNK is 0.

Do not compare val loss

BPE val loss is per BPE symbol and word-level val loss is per word. Different units, so compare the two systems by BLEU only.

Merges are not in the checkpoint

best.pt stores the vocab and config, so both models load without the train data. The English merges are not stored. We re-learn them (deterministic) to segment the test source with segment(word, rules_map), which adds </w>.

The gap is not a decoding artifact

Greedy decoding traps the word-level model in <UNK> loops, so part of the greedy gap could have come from that. But beam search adds ~+3.5 to both systems and the gap stays at 0.86. So the gain comes from the vocabulary.

Where the gain sits

Mostly in unigram precision (+3.6 greedy, +1.9 beam); higher n-grams move less. That fits BPE: it gets more individual words right, while the architecture, and with it word order, is the same.

Bugs that changed the numbers
  • BPE BLEU 0.01: is_bpe=False, and source words segmented without </w>. Use segment(word, rules_map).
  • Reference from ids: <UNK> in both hypothesis and reference matched, inflating BLEU. Score against the original tokens.
  • Lost tokenize="none" in a refactor: a second tokenizer changed the score.
  • Table 1 v1: types returned as a set, UNK counted per unique word instead of per occurrence.
